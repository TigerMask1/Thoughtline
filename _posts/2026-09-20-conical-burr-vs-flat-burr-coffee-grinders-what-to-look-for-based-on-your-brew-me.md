Both crush coffee beans into small particles far better than any blade grinder, but they produce fundamentally different grounds. The shape of the cutting surface dictates the grind distribution—how many different-sized specks of coffee come out of the chute—and that directly impacts how water extracts flavor from the coffee bed.\n\nUnderstanding how each burr style behaves helps you match the grinder to the gear on your kitchen counter.\n\n## The Mechanical Difference: Bimodal vs. Unimodal\n\nA [conical burr set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burr_mill) consists of an inner cone-shaped cutting ring that sits inside an outer serrated ring. Beans fall vertically into the gap, getting crushed progressively smaller as they drop. Conical burrs naturally generate a **bimodal grind distribution**. That means the grinder produces two distinct particle spikes: the target size you selected, plus a secondary cluster of microscopic dust called \"fines.\"\n\nFlat burrs consist of two identical ring-shaped discs sitting parallel to each other, flat face to flat face. Beans enter the center, and centrifugal force flings them outward between teeth that get progressively tighter toward the outer rim. Flat burrs produce a **unimodal distribution**, meaning nearly all coffee grounds come out at a uniform size, with far fewer fines.\n\nNeither profile is objectively superior. Fines slow down water flow and add body; uniform grinds allow high extraction with intense flavor clarity.\n\n## Pour-Over and Drip: The Flat Burr Advantage\n\nIf manual pour-over (like a V60, Kalita Wave, or Chemex) or automatic drip is your primary morning ritual, flat burrs generally offer the upper hand.\n\nBecause flat burrs minimize fines, water drains evenly through the bed without clogging filter paper. When fines clog the paper, brew water pools on top, leading to over-extraction and a harsh, astringent finish.
