A used solid wood dining table can last generations, but only if you know what to check before paying and loading it into your vehicle. Listings frequently claim a piece is "solid oak" or "solid walnut" when it is actually wood veneer over particleboard, or structural joints are failing under the surface. Here is a practical, step-by-step checklist to inspect a used table in person before handing over your money.

## 1. Verify It Is Genuine Solid Wood

Sellers often confuse real solid lumber with [wood veneer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Veneering)—a thin slice of real wood glued over MDF or particleboard. To confirm you are buying genuine solid wood:

- **Check the end grain:** Look closely at the short edge of the tabletop. On a solid wood board, the growth rings will curve across the thickness of the edge. If you see a flat seam, tape-like banding, or a pattern that suddenly stops, it is edge-banding glued over a composite core.
- **Inspect the underside:** Flip your view beneath the table. Solid wood boards will show the same grain characteristics (knots, cathedral patterns, and grain direction) on the underside directly beneath where they appear on top.
- **Feel the weight:** A 6-foot solid oak, maple, or walnut dining table usually weighs between 100 and 180 pounds. If you can lift one end effortlessly with two fingers, the table is likely lightweight composite or hollow-core construction.

## 2. Test Joinery and Frame Rigidity

Never buy a table without testing its structural integrity. Place both hands on diagonally opposite corners of the top and give it a firm push back and forth.

- **Inspect corner brackets:** Look underneath where the legs connect to the apron (the vertical frame running beneath the top). Quality solid wood tables use metal corner brackets with heavy hanger bolts, or solid wood blocks secured with lag screws. Check whether these screws are stripped, rusted, or loose.
- **Examine the leg joints:** Look for traditional mortise-and-tenon joints where the apron meets the legs. If the glue has failed from age or stress, you will notice hairline gaps or white, flaking adhesive in the joints.
- **Look for expansion joints:** Quality plank tables often feature [breadboard ends](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breadboard_end)—narrow boards running perpendicular across the ends of the table top to keep planks flat. Check that these joints are properly fitted and not split from improper gluing.

## 3. Check for Warping, Cupping, and Sagging

Solid wood reacts to changes in ambient humidity. Storing a table in a damp basement, unheated garage, or humid storage facility can cause permanent warping.

- **Sight down the top:** Crouch down until your eyes are level with the edge of the tabletop and look straight down its full length. Look for dips, twisting, or sagging in the middle.
- **Test for cupping:** Lay a straight ruler or a 4-foot spirit level flat across the width of the top. Cupping happens when individual planks curl upward at their edges. A gap greater than 1/8 inch under the center of your level means plates and drink glasses will rock back and forth when set down.

## 4. Distinguish Minor Finish Wear from Deep Damage

Don't walk away from a solid table just because the top looks dull, but adjust your offer based on the labor needed to fix it.

- **Light surface scratches:** Clear coat scratches in polyurethane or lacquer can usually be buffed out or covered with a fresh coat of wipe-on finish or paste wax.
- **White rings versus black stains:** White water rings are trapped moisture inside the topcoat finish, which can be drawn out with low heat or gentle abrasive polish. Dark brown or black spots mean water reached the raw wood grain, which requires chemical stripping, sanding, and wood bleach (oxalic acid) to repair.
- **Flaking topcoat:** Flaking lacquer requires stripping the entire tabletop down to raw wood. Account for roughly 6 to 10 hours of labor and $40 to $70 in sandpaper, stripper, and topcoat supplies when negotiating price.

## 5. Measure Hardware and Transport Clearances

Before making an offer, make sure you can physically disassemble and transport the table.

- **Test leg removal:** Crawl underneath with a flashlight. Most quality dining tables use 1/2-inch or 9/16-inch nuts holding the legs into corner brackets. Bring a socket wrench set to verify the nuts turn freely and aren't rusted tight or rounded off.
- **Check chair and leg clearance:** Standard dining tabletop height is 29 to 30 inches off the floor, with at least 24 inches of apron clearance for legroom. Measure between the legs to ensure your existing chairs will fit—each chair requires 22 to 24 inches of width to slide in comfortably.
