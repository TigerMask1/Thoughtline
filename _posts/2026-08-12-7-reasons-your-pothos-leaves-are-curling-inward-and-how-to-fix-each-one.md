Pothos plants ([Epipremnum aureum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epipremnum_aureum)) are notoriously forgiving, but when their lush leaves start curling inward into tight tubes, the plant is signaling stress. Leaf curling is usually a defensive reflex: the plant reduces its surface area to slow down water loss or protect itself from environmental shock.

Here are seven specific reasons your pothos leaves are curling inward and concrete steps to fix each one.

## 1. Underwatering (Severe Dehydration)

When pothos soil dries out completely, the plant curls its leaves inward to conserve whatever moisture remains in its tissues.

* **The Signs:** The top 2 to 3 inches of soil feel bone-dry, the pot feels lightweight, and the curling leaves feel thin or crisp.
* **The Fix:** Perform a thorough bottom-watering. Place the pot in a tub or basin filled with 2 to 3 inches of lukewarm water for 30 to 45 minutes, allowing the soil to draw moisture up through the bottom drainage holes. Let all excess water drain before returning the pot to its saucer.

## 2. Overwatering and Early Root Rot

Counterintuitively, overwatering causes inward curling too. Rotting roots lose the ability to absorb water and transport it to the foliage, leaving the leaves functionally dehydrated despite sitting in wet soil.

* **The Signs:** Soil stays soggy for more than 7 to 10 days, leaves feel soft or limp, and lower foliage turns yellow.
* **The Fix:** Slide the plant out of its pot to inspect the roots. Trim away any black, mushy roots using shears disinfected with rubbing alcohol. Repot the plant into fresh, aerated soil—a mix of 70% standard potting soil and 30% perlite works well—and make sure the container has clear drainage holes.

## 3. Low Ambient Humidity

Pothos prefer humidity levels between 40% and 60%. During winter, indoor heating can drop humidity below 30%, causing the plant's leaf edges to curl inward to trap ambient moisture.

* **The Signs:** Leaf edges curl inward while the leaves remain green and the soil moisture level feels normal.
* **The Fix:** Move the plant away from forced-air vents. Group it near other moisture-releasing houseplants, set the pot on a shallow tray filled with pebbles and water (keep the pot base dry above the waterline), or run a small cool-mist humidifier in the room.

## 4. Temperature Extremes and Drafts

Pothos thrive best in temperatures between 65°F and 85°F (18°C to 29°C). Exposure to cold drafts below 55°F (13°C) or hot air blasts causes stress that leads to foliage curling.

* **The Signs:** Curling affects only one side of the plant, usually the side facing a drafty window, exterior door, or radiator.
* **The Fix:** Relocate the plant to a spot with stable temperatures, at least 4 to 5 feet away from drafty window frames, air conditioning units, and heating vents.

## 5. Sap-Sucking Pests

Pests like thrips, spider mites, and mealybugs pierce individual leaf cells to feed on sap. This damage disrupts water distribution within the leaf, causing it to distort and roll inward.

* **The Signs:** Fine web-like strands on the undersides of leaves, sticky sap residue on stems, or tiny silver/yellow stippling on leaf surfaces.
* **The Fix:** Isolate the plant from other houseplants immediately. Wipe down the stems and both sides of every leaf with a wet cloth. Spray the foliage thoroughly with diluted [neem oil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neem_oil) or insecticidal soap, repeating the treatment every 7 days for three weeks.

## 6. Fertilizer Burn (Salt Buildup)

Applying liquid fertilizer too frequently or using full-strength synthetic mixes damages sensitive root tips. Fertilizer salts build up in the potting medium and extract water from the roots through reverse osmosis.

* **The Signs:** A white, crusty film on top of the soil surface, alongside leaves that display brown, burnt tips and inward curling.
* **The Fix:** Flush the pot with room-temperature tap or distilled water for 2 to 3 minutes, allowing excess liquid to drain freely out the bottom. Pause fertilizing for 8 to 10 weeks, then resume at half the recommended dosage only during spring and summer.

## 7. The Plant Is Severely Rootbound

When a pothos fills its pot with a dense web of roots, little soil remains to hold moisture. The roots dry out rapidly between waterings, triggering consistent leaf curling.

* **The Signs:** Roots winding around the surface, dense root masses poking through bottom drainage holes, or water running straight through the pot without soaking into the soil.
* **The Fix:** Select a new container that is 2 inches wider in diameter than the current pot. Unpot the plant, gently tease apart the root ball to loosen tightly wound roots, and repot with fresh, nutrient-rich potting mix.
