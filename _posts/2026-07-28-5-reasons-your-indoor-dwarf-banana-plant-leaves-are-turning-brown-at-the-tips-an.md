Dwarf banana plants like cultivars of [Musa acuminata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musa_acuminata) bring a dramatic, tropical aesthetic indoors, but their massive leaves act as early warning systems when growing conditions slip out of balance. Crispy brown leaf tips are usually the first sign that your plant is under environmental stress. Because bananas are heavy drinkers that require high ambient moisture, indoor living spaces present several challenges. Here are the five most common reasons the tips of your dwarf banana leaves are turning brown, along with concrete steps to restore your plant to health.

## 1. Low Relative Humidity

Dwarf bananas are native to tropical rainforest environments where relative humidity regularly hovers above 60%. Most homes, particularly during winter when heating systems are running, drop below 30% humidity. When ambient air is dry, moisture leaves the broad leaf surface faster than the roots can supply water, causing the delicate cells at the very edges and tips to die and turn crisp.

**How to fix it:** Aim for a relative humidity between 50% and 70%. Monitor your room using an inexpensive digital hygrometer. The most effective fix is running a cool-mist humidifier placed 3 to 4 feet from the plant. You can also group your banana plant closely with other tropical houseplants to create a humid microclimate, or set the pot on a tray filled with pebbles and water (making sure the base of the pot sits on the pebbles above the water line to avoid root rot).

## 2. Under-Watering or Irregular Moisture

Because of their large surface area, banana leaves undergo high rates of [transpiration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transpiration). If the soil dries out completely, the plant cuts off water delivery to its leaf extremities first to protect its central pseudostem. This quickly results in papery brown tips and yellowing edges.

**How to fix it:** Keep the soil consistently moist, but never soggy. Check the top 1 to 2 inches of soil with your finger every few days. When that top layer feels dry, water the plant thoroughly until liquid flows freely out of the bottom drainage holes. Always empty the drainage saucer 15 minutes after watering so the roots do not sit in standing water. During peak spring and summer growth, an indoor dwarf banana in a 10-inch pot may require deep watering two to three times per week.

## 3. Mineral and Salt Accumulation from Tap Water

If you water your plant using hard municipal tap water, minerals such as calcium, chlorine, and fluoride accumulate in the soil mix over time. As the plant absorbs water, these dissolved salts travel through the vascular system and concentrate at the tips of the leaves. Once the water evaporates from the leaf edges, the remaining salts burn the delicate leaf tissue.

**How to fix it:** Switch to using gathered rainwater, distilled water, or filtered water. If tap water is your only option, flush the soil every three months: place the pot in a sink or shower and slowly pour a volume of tap water equal to twice the pot size through the soil to leach out built-up salts. Allow all excess water to drain thoroughly before returning the plant to its spot.

## 4. Over-Fertilizing

Dwarf bananas are heavy feeders during the spring and summer, but excess fertilizer introduces too many nitrogen compounds and mineral salts into the soil mix. This burns the fine root tips underground, preventing the plant from absorbing water efficiently and leading to severe brown tipping on the foliage.

**How to fix it:** Only fertilize when the plant is actively growing between April and September. Use a balanced liquid houseplant fertilizer (such as a 10-10-10 ratio) diluted to half the manufacturer's recommended strength once every two to three weeks. Never apply fertilizer to bone-dry soil, as concentrated salts will burn dry roots instantly; always moisten the soil with plain water first.

## 5. Cold Drafts and Rapid Temperature Drops

Dwarf banana plants thrive in warm temperatures between 65°F and 85°F (18°C to 29°C). Exposing the plant to temperatures below 55°F (13°C), or positioning it in the path of cold air currents, causes thermal shock. Drafts disrupt the plant's internal water transport, causing leaf tips to turn dark brown or black within days.

**How to fix it:** Move your plant away from drafty exterior doors, single-pane windows during winter, air conditioning units, and forced-air heating vents. Select a spot that offers bright, indirect light—such as a few feet back from a sunny south- or east-facing window—where ambient temperatures remain stable day and night.
