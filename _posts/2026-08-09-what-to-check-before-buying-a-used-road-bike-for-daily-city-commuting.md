Buying a used [road bike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Road_bicycle) for daily city commuting can save you hundreds of dollars, but aggressive racing setups don't always handle city potholes, rain, and stop-and-go traffic well. Before handing over cash to a seller, you need to verify that the frame is structurally sound, the geometry works for urban riding, and the components won't require immediate, expensive repairs. Use this step-by-step checklist to evaluate a used bike before you buy.

## Frame Integrity and Fit

A damaged frame is unsafe, and an improperly sized bike will make daily riding painful.

- **Inspect frame tubes and welds:** Run your fingers along the underside of the top tube and down tube, especially near the front fork junction. On aluminum frames, look closely for fine hairline cracks in the paint around weld joints. On steel frames, look for bubbling paint, which indicates internal rust. It is best to avoid carbon fiber frames for budget commuting; carbon is susceptible to hidden stress fractures from drop-bar impacts at crowded bike racks.
- **Check sizing:** Stand over the top tube with your feet flat on the ground. You want roughly 1 to 2 inches of clearance above the bar. When sitting on the saddle with your foot at the bottom of the pedal stroke, your knee should retain a slight 25 to 30-degree bend. If you have to stretch your arms out completely straight to reach the handlebars, the top tube is too long for comfortable city maneuvering.

## Tire Clearance and Commuter Mounts

Traditional road bikes often use skinny tires and lack attachment points. City riding requires a more versatile setup.

- **Check tire clearance:** City pavement demands wider tires to absorb road vibration and prevent pinch flats. Inspect the clearance inside the front fork, rear chainstays, and underneath the brake calipers. Make sure the frame can fit at least a 28mm or 32mm tire. If 23mm tires are currently installed with only a millimeter of clearance to spare against the brakes, you won't be able to upgrade to wider rubber.
- **Look for frame eyelets:** Check near the rear wheel dropouts and upper seatstays for small threaded screw holes (braze-ons). These eyelets allow you to bolt on full-coverage fenders and a rear cargo rack for panniers. If they are missing, you will have to rely on clip-on fenders and carry your gear in a backpack.

## Drivetrain and Brake Wear

Replacing a worn [drivetrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicycle_drivetrain_systems) can quickly add $100 to $200 in parts alone, wiping out any savings from buying used.

- **Measure chain stretch:** A new bike chain measures exactly 12 inches across 12 full links (pin to pin). Hold a metal ruler against the chain. If 12 links measure 12 and 1/16 inches, the chain is stretched and needs replacement. If the measurement reaches 12 and 1/8 inches or more, the chain has worn down the rear cassette teeth, meaning both parts must be replaced.
- **Check gear teeth shapes:** Look closely at the rear cassette cogs and front chainrings. Healthy gear teeth have flat, blunted tops. If the teeth look sharp and curved like shark fins, the drivetrain is heavily worn and the chain will slip under hard pedaling.
- **Examine the brake tracks:** Rub your thumb across the aluminum braking track on the wheel rims. If the metal feels concave or hollowed out from pad friction, the sidewall is worn thin and the wheel rim is nearing structural failure.

## Bearings and the Test Ride

Before making an offer, check the hidden moving components and take a test ride under realistic riding conditions.

- **Check the headset and bottom bracket:** Squeeze the front brake lever firmly and rock the bike forward and backward. If you feel a knocking or clunking sensation in the handlebars, the headset bearings are loose or pitted. Next, grab a crank arm and try to wiggle it side-to-side toward the frame; any play indicates a worn bottom bracket.
- **Spin the wheels:** Lift each wheel and give it a hard spin. It should rotate smoothly without grinding noises. Watch the tire pass between the brake pads—if it wobbles side-to-side by more than 1 to 2 millimeters, the wheel needs to be trued.
- **Perform a load test:** Ride the bike up a slight incline and shift through every gear while pedaling with moderate force. Gears should engage cleanly without skipping or popping off the sprockets. Test both brakes from a moderate speed; stopping power should feel firm and progressive, without squealing or bringing the brake levers all the way to the handlebar grips.
