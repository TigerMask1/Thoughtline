Learn 4 key signs to diagnose root rot vs. transplant shock and save your plant.", "image_query": "drooping peace lily plant", "article_markdown": "A drooping [Spathiphyllum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spathiphyllum) (peace lily) usually means one thing: the plant needs water. But when you touch the soil and find it thoroughly wet, adding more water will only speed up its death. 

When a peace lily droops despite wet soil, the leaves are dehydrated because the roots are physically unable to draw water up into the stems. This failure stems from one of two causes: severe root damage (usually [root rot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_rot) from overwatering) or acute transplant shock after repotting. Identifying which issue you are facing determines whether your plant needs gentle patience or emergency surgery.

Here are the 4 key signs to distinguish root rot from transplant shock, along with specific steps to fix each.

## 1. Timeline and Event History

The fastest way to narrow down the cause is looking at the calendar.

*   **Transplant Shock:** Symptoms appear rapidly, usually within 24 to 72 hours after you repot the plant, divide its root ball, or move it to a drastically different environment. The sudden change disrupts the fine root hairs responsible for moisture absorption.
*   **Root Damage (Rot):** Rot develops slowly over several weeks or months. It is the cumulative result of keeping the soil continuously saturated, using a pot without drainage holes, or letting the plant sit in a saucer full of standing water for days at a time.

## 2. Leaf Color and Texture

Both conditions cause leaves to go limp, but the secondary foliage symptoms look very different.

*   **Transplant Shock:** The leaves droop completely flat, but they usually stay dark green and pliable for the first several days. You won't see immediate yellowing or soft, decaying spots. The plant simply looks exhausted and wilted.
*   **Root Damage (Rot):** Leaves develop bright yellow halos, starting with the lower, older leaves. As the infection progresses, leaf tips turn soft brown or black—not crisp like underwatered tips, but mushy. Stems near the soil line may turn black and collapse at the base.

## 3. Soil Odor and Texture

The smell and structure of the potting medium provide direct clues about what is happening beneath the surface.

*   **Transplant Shock:** The soil smells fresh, earthy, or neutral—like fresh potting mix. Water moves through the pot normally when you test it.
*   **Root Damage (Rot):** The soil gives off a sour, swampy, or rotten smell caused by anaerobic bacteria thriving in oxygen-starved soil. The soil often remains dense, heavy, and muddy, failing to dry out even after 7 to 10 days in a warm room.

## 4. Root System Appearance (The Final Test)

If you aren't certain based on the top growth, slide the plant out of its pot to inspect the roots directly.

*   **Transplant Shock:** The roots are intact, firm, and white or light tan. You may see a few broken roots from repotting, but the overall root mass feels springy and solid.
*   **Root Damage (Rot):** Damaged roots look dark brown or black, feel slimy, and fall apart when touched. If you pull gently on an affected root, the mushy outer sheath slides right off, leaving behind a thin, stringy inner core.

## How to Save Your Plant Based on the Diagnosis

### If it is Transplant Shock:
Do not keep adding water to "help" a shocked plant—wet roots that aren't absorbing water will quickly rot. Instead:
1.  Move the plant out of direct sunlight into bright, indirect light.
2.  Increase ambient humidity. Place a clear plastic bag loosely over the top of the plant for 3 to 5 days to create a mini-greenhouse, reducing moisture loss through the leaves.
3.  Hold off on fertilizing for at least 4 to 6 weeks. Fertilizer salts will burn delicate new root growth.
4.  Allow the top 1 to 2 inches of soil to dry out slightly before watering again. Most peace lilies recover from transplant shock within 7 to 14 days.

### If it is Root Rot:
You must act immediately before fungal pathogens kill the crown of the plant.
1.  Remove the plant from its pot and gently wash away all old soil under lukewarm tap water.
2.  Using clean shears wiped down with 70% isopropyl alcohol, prune away all soft, black, or slimy roots until only healthy, firm tissue remains.
3.  Trim off any heavily yellowed or dead leaves to reduce the burden on the smaller root mass.
4.  Repot the peace lily in a fresh, well-draining mix—a combination of 60% standard potting soil, 20% perlite, and 20% orchid bark works extremely well. 
5.  Ensure the new pot has functional drainage holes, and refrain from heavy watering for the first week to allow root wounds to callous over."}
