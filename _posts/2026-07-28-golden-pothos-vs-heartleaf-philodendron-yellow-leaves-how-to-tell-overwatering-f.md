Golden pothos and heartleaf philodendron are often confused for one another, and for good reason: both are trailing tropical vines with heart-shaped leaves and easygoing natures. However, when yellow leaves appear, treating them as identical plants can lead to the wrong diagnosis. Their leaf thickness and stem anatomy differ, which changes how they show stress from too much or too little water.

## First: Confirm Your Plant Type

Before diagnosing leaf color, make sure you know which plant you are holding:

- **Golden Pothos ([Epipremnum aureum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epipremnum_aureum)):** Has thicker, waxier leaves with a slightly asymmetrical base. The stem attached to each leaf (the petiole) has a distinct groove or ridge running down its side.
- **Heartleaf Philodendron ([Philodendron hederaceum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philodendron_hederaceum)):** Has thinner, softer leaves with a sharp point and a symmetrical heart shape. The petioles are smooth and rounded, and new leaves emerge from papery sheaths called cataphylls, which dry up and turn brown on the stem.

## Golden Pothos: Overwatering vs. Underwatering

Because pothos leaves are thick and store moderate moisture, their symptoms remain distinct for several days before a leaf falls off.

**Signs of Overwatered Golden Pothos:**
- **Texture:** Yellow leaves feel soft, limp, or slightly swollen and mushy rather than dry.
- **Pattern:** Yellowing begins near the base of the plant (closest to the soil) on older leaves. You may notice dark brown or black water-soaked spots in the center of the leaf surrounded by a bright yellow halo.
- **Soil Condition:** Soil remains wet or heavy 2 to 3 inches deep more than 7 to 10 days after watering.

**Signs of Underwatered Golden Pothos:**
- **Texture:** Yellow leaves feel thin, dry, and papery. Tips and leaf edges curl inward and turn crispy brown.
- **Pattern:** Entire vines lose tension and droop before turning yellow. Yellowing can occur on both old and new leaves across the plant.
- **Soil Condition:** Soil is bone dry 3 inches down, feels light, and may have pulled away from the inner edges of the pot.

## Heartleaf Philodendron: Overwatering vs. Underwatering

Heartleaf philodendrons have thinner foliage than pothos, making them react faster to moisture changes.

**Signs of Overwatered Heartleaf Philodendron:**
- **Texture:** Leaves turn a pale, washed-out yellow quickly and drop easily when touched. They feel flimsy and wet.
- **Pattern:** The brown cataphyll sheaths near the leaf stems turn black and slimy instead of dry and papery. Soft brown decay spreads quickly from the leaf stem into the leaf base.
- **Soil Condition:** The top inch of soil smells sour or stale, and stem bases at the soil line look dark or pinched.

**Signs of Underwatered Heartleaf Philodendron:**
- **Texture:** Leaves turn bright yellow, curl tightly into narrow tubes to conserve water, and become brittle.
- **Pattern:** Lower leaves turn pale yellow or light tan and drop in groups. Aerial root nodes look shriveled and dry.
- **Soil Condition:** Water runs straight down the sides of the pot without soaking into the center because the potting mix has become hydrophobic.

## Quick Recovery Action Steps

1. **For Overwatering:** Stop watering immediately. Check drainage holes for blocked soil. If soil remains wet past 10 days, gently remove the plant from its pot to inspect roots. Healthy roots are firm and white or tan; rotted roots are black, stringy, and smell foul. Trim rotted roots with sanitized shears and repot into a mix containing 30% perlite or coarse orchid bark for aeration.
2. **For Underwatering:** Give the plant a deep soak. If the soil is repelling water, submerge the bottom half of the pot in a tub of lukewarm water for 30 minutes until the topsoil feels damp, then let it drain completely.
3. **Schedule Adjustment:** Only water when the top 2 inches of soil feel dry for a golden pothos, or when the top 1 to 1.5 inches feel dry for a heartleaf philodendron.
