Seeing your [Monstera deliciosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monstera_deliciosa) droop within 24 to 48 hours of repotting is alarming. Before you assume the worst and pull the plant back out of its container, take a step back. A freshly repotted Monstera often droops due to short-term environmental stress rather than a fatal root disease. Distinguishing between standard transplant shock and actual root rot is the key to taking the right corrective action.

## Cause 1: Standard Transplant Shock

When you unpot a Monstera, loosen its root ball, or tease apart tangled roots, microscopic root hairs break. These delicate structures absorb up to 90% of the plant's water. Until the plant grows new root hairs—usually within 5 to 10 days—it cannot draw up moisture at its normal rate, even if the soil is damp.

* **Signs:** Stems remain mostly upright, but the large leaves look wilted or saggy. The soil is damp to the touch.
* **Fix:** Keep the plant in stable room temperatures (65–75°F) with medium, indirect light. Do not apply fertilizer for at least four weeks, as fertilizer salts can scorch newly damaged root tips.

## Cause 2: Hidden Air Pockets in the Soil

If you did not gently settle fresh soil around the root mass during potting, large air pockets can leave sections of roots suspended in dry space. Water flows straight past these voids without actually saturating the core root ball, causing rapid dehydration despite recent watering.

* **Signs:** The pot feels unusually light when lifted, water drains out the bottom instantly, and leaves wilt while the top layer of soil seems moist.
* **Fix:** Water slowly and thoroughly around the entire perimeter of the pot until water runs freely out of the drainage holes. Lightly tap the sides of the pot to help the potting mix settle snugly around the roots.

## Cause 3: Over-Potting (Moving to a Pot That Is Too Large)

Jumping more than 2 inches in pot diameter (for instance, moving directly from an 8-inch pot to a 12-inch or 14-inch pot) leaves a large volume of soil unpenetrated by roots. This excess soil holds water far longer than the plant can absorb it, depriving roots of oxygen and causing temporary wilting.

* **Signs:** Soil stays dark, heavy, and wet for more than 7 to 10 days; leaves droop continuously.
* **Fix:** Repot into a container that is only 1 to 2 inches wider than the root ball. Ensure your soil mix includes plenty of chunky amendments—a 1:1:1 ratio of indoor potting soil, coarse perlite, and orchid bark ensures adequate airflow.

## Cause 4: Immediate Light and Environmental Stress

Moving your Monstera to a brighter spot immediately after repotting forces its leaves to lose water through transpiration faster than damaged roots can replenish it.

* **Signs:** Leaves curl inward along the edges in addition to drooping, especially during warmer afternoon hours.
* **Fix:** Place the plant 3 to 5 feet back from direct sun or bright windows for 7 to 14 days after repotting to let root function recover before moving it back to high-light spots.

## How to Identify Actual Root Rot

Unlike simple transplant shock, root rot is an active fungal or bacterial infection that turns roots mushy and prevents water transport entirely. It typically occurs when a plant sits in soggy, poorly draining soil over an extended period.

* **Odor:** Transplant shock smells like clean dirt. Root rot produces a distinct sour, swampy, or decaying odor from the drainage holes.
* **Leaf Pattern:** Shock causes uniform wilting across green leaves. Root rot causes yellowing lower leaves with dark brown or black soft spots, often surrounded by a yellow halo.
* **Stem Texture:** In shock, stems feel firm to the touch. With root rot, stems near the soil line feel soft, hollow, or squishy.
* **Duration:** Shock improves noticeably within 7 to 10 days. Root rot gets progressively worse after a week, regardless of light or air conditions.

If you confirm root rot, gently remove the plant from its pot and wash away the soil. Trim off all black, mushy, or stringy roots using clean shears disinfected with rubbing alcohol. Spray the remaining healthy white roots with a 3% hydrogen peroxide solution, then repot into a clean pot filled with fresh, porous soil.
